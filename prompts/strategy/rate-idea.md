# Execute: Rate an Idea for Commercial Feasibility

## Objective

Evaluate a business, product, service, or venture idea as an investment and execution decision. Deliver a rigorous, skeptical, evidence-based go/no-go assessment.

The goal is not to encourage the user or make the idea sound attractive. The goal is to determine whether a realistic path exists to a commercially meaningful, profitable business, what it would take, how long it would take, and what could make it fail.

Be direct and pragmatic. Do not flatter the idea, soften negative conclusions, or confuse an interesting concept with a viable business. Equally, do not dismiss an idea without examining evidence and plausible routes to market.

## Mandatory opening sentence

The report must begin with this sentence, populated with explicit values:

"I give it a [X]% chance of it reaching [Y] MRR in [Z] months."

- X is the estimated probability, expressed as a percentage, of reaching the stated target.
- Y is a specific monthly recurring revenue target in a stated currency (for example, €10,000 MRR or RON 50,000 MRR). Do not express the revenue amount as a percentage.
- Z is a specific number of months from the assumed start date.
- If the user has not supplied a target MRR or timeframe, choose defensible targets based on the idea and explain them immediately after the opening sentence. Do not ask for these details if reasonable assumptions can be made.
- The probability is an analytical judgment, not a statistically validated forecast. Explain its basis, key assumptions, and uncertainty. Never imply false precision.

## Research standard: use all relevant available resources

Use the resources actually available in the current session and relevant to the idea. This can include:

- Current web research and primary sources.
- Official demographic, economic, labor, trade, business-registration, and statistical datasets.
- Government publications, regulators, industry associations, and procurement data.
- Market research, analyst reports, academic research, and credible industry publications.
- Competitor websites, pricing pages, product documentation, customer reviews, app stores, marketplaces, and public company disclosures.
- Search trends, audience signals, communities, forums, and social platforms where accessible.
- Relevant files, project sources, previous work in the current project, and connected business or analytics systems where available and authorized.
- Relevant prior decisions or constraints explicitly available in the current project context.

Search broadly enough to answer the decision, but prioritize high-quality evidence over a large pile of weak sources. Use recent data for current market conditions and label older sources. Cite factual claims and provide links. Do not claim to have checked a source, database, connector, or dataset unless you actually accessed it.

If a source is unavailable, say so. Do not fabricate market statistics, competitor metrics, customer demand, conversion rates, or research findings. Separate verified facts, estimates, assumptions, and hypotheses throughout.

## Analysis workflow

### 1. Define the idea and the commercial objective

- Restate the idea in concrete terms: customer, problem, proposed solution, value delivered, business model, and likely purchase trigger.
- Identify the paying customer and distinguish users, beneficiaries, buyers, and decision-makers.
- Identify the strongest assumptions on which commercial success depends.
- Define the proposed MRR target, timeframe, currency, geography, and what counts as success.
- If the user has supplied relevant details in the current project or conversation, use them rather than asking again.
- Ask a clarification only if the idea itself is too vague to assess meaningfully. Otherwise state assumptions and proceed.

### 2. Market selection and best market fit

Compare plausible launch and expansion markets. Consider, where relevant:

- Romania.
- Wider Europe, including EU/EEA distinctions where they matter.
- United Kingdom and other relevant individual countries.
- United States and other global markets.
- Global opportunity, while distinguishing theoretical demand from realistically addressable demand.

Do not assume that the largest market is the best market. Evaluate each geography against:

- Number and characteristics of potential customers, using relevant demographics and business counts.
- Problem intensity, frequency, urgency, and current alternatives.
- Ability and willingness to pay; purchasing power and local price tolerance.
- Market maturity, adoption behavior, digital penetration, and relevant infrastructure.
- Competitor density, incumbent strength, fragmentation, and underserved segments.
- Language, localization, cultural, sales, and support requirements.
- Regulation, licensing, tax, privacy, consumer protection, and cross-border constraints.
- Customer acquisition channels, distribution access, and likely acquisition cost.
- Payment methods, procurement cycles, and sales-cycle length.
- Operational complexity and ability to serve customers from the user's likely operating base.

Provide a comparative market table and rank the best initial market, secondary markets, and markets to avoid initially. Explain why. Recommend a beachhead segment and geography rather than vaguely recommending "global".

### 3. Market size and demand validation

Estimate TAM, SAM, and realistically obtainable SOM where defensible.

- Show the calculation, source, year, geography, and assumptions behind each estimate.
- Prefer bottom-up sizing (number of reachable target customers × realistic annual spend) over unsupported top-down market-report figures.
- Avoid treating the entire population or broad industry spend as the addressable market.
- Assess evidence of demand: existing spend, search behavior, competitor traction, customer complaints, workarounds, procurement activity, and willingness-to-pay signals.
- Identify what remains unproven and the cheapest credible tests to validate it.
- If the market cannot be sized credibly, state that and provide a validation plan instead of inventing a number.

### 4. Customer, positioning, and competition

- Define priority customer segments and the initial ideal customer profile.
- Describe the job-to-be-done, pain, current workaround, switching barriers, and purchase decision.
- Map direct competitors, indirect competitors, substitutes, and the option of doing nothing.
- Compare product scope, pricing, positioning, distribution, strengths, weaknesses, and evidence of traction where available.
- Identify a credible differentiation and explain whether it is defensible or easily copied.
- Assess likely pricing, packaging, willingness to pay, and monetization model.
- Identify why customers would buy now, why they might not, and what would make them switch.

### 5. Product scope and development plan

- Define the smallest viable product that can test the commercial thesis, not an unnecessarily complete product.
- Separate must-have launch capabilities from later features.
- Estimate development phases, calendar duration, effort, and dependencies.
- State assumptions about team size, skills, outsourcing, integrations, compliance, QA, and user testing.
- Provide a phase-by-phase timeline with deliverables and decision gates, such as discovery, validation, prototype, MVP, pilot, launch, and iteration.
- Estimate one-time development costs in the relevant currency, separating cash expenditure from founder/team opportunity cost.
- Identify the critical path, technical risks, and likely scope traps.
- Include a realistic contingency buffer. Do not assume an ideal uninterrupted development schedule.

### 6. Revenue model and scenarios

Build at least three scenarios: conservative, base, and upside.

For each scenario, show a monthly or milestone-based path from launch to the target horizon, including:

- Customers or accounts acquired.
- Leads, traffic, trials, or sales opportunities required.
- Conversion assumptions at each meaningful funnel stage.
- Price, plan mix, average revenue per account, and MRR.
- Churn, retention, repeat purchase, expansion, and seasonality where relevant.
- Sales-cycle length and time to revenue.
- Gross margin and revenue recognition assumptions where relevant.

Use explicit formulas and show how the target MRR could be reached. Distinguish recurring revenue from one-off revenue. For non-subscription businesses, define an appropriate monthly revenue equivalent and explain why the MRR framing is not directly applicable.

Do not create optimistic hockey-stick curves without an acquisition mechanism. Tie customer growth to channel capacity, sales productivity, conversion rates, budget, and realistic market penetration.

### 7. Operating costs and cash requirements

Estimate one-time setup costs and ongoing monthly operating costs. Include relevant items such as:

- Hosting, cloud, APIs, AI/model usage, software, storage, and infrastructure.
- Payment processing, refunds, fraud, and chargebacks.
- Customer support, operations, contractors, and employee costs including employer taxes.
- Accounting, legal, tax, insurance, compliance, and administration.
- Content, tooling, integrations, and third-party services.
- Sales commissions, partnerships, and customer success.
- Marketing and advertising, reported separately as well as included in total operating costs.

Show lean, expected, and scale-up cost scenarios. State what costs are fixed, variable, or usage-based. Identify costs that grow with customers or revenue and likely step-changes in staffing or infrastructure.

Estimate initial cash required, monthly burn before break-even, working-capital needs, and a sensible contingency reserve. For Romania and other jurisdictions, use appropriate local cost and tax assumptions and clearly label any tax treatment that requires professional confirmation.

### 8. Marketing, acquisition, and launch budget

Recommend a practical go-to-market strategy for the selected beachhead market.

- Identify priority channels and explain why each is appropriate for the customer and buying process.
- Assess organic, paid, outbound, partnerships, affiliates, marketplaces, SEO/content, community, PR, and direct sales where relevant.
- Estimate the initial test budget, monthly budget by phase, and total marketing budget through the target horizon.
- Provide an advertising and launch timeline, including pre-launch research, waitlist or lead capture, pilot, launch, optimization, and scaling.
- Estimate CAC by channel or scenario where evidence permits. Include assumptions for CPM/CPC, conversion rates, lead-to-customer rates, sales costs, and payback period where applicable.
- Define measurable campaign KPIs and stop/continue/scale thresholds.
- Explain which channels should not be funded initially and why.

Do not present generic advice such as "use social media". Tie budget and channel choices to the customer, geography, price point, and sales cycle.

### 9. Profitability and unit economics

Evaluate commercial viability, not just revenue potential.

Calculate or estimate, with formulas and assumptions:

- Gross profit and gross margin.
- Contribution margin after variable delivery and acquisition costs.
- CAC, LTV, LTV:CAC, and CAC payback where the business model supports them.
- Monthly operating profit/loss and EBITDA-like operating result where meaningful.
- Break-even revenue and customer count.
- Expected time to monthly break-even.
- Cumulative cash flow and total cash required before break-even.
- Founder compensation assumptions and whether the business is only "profitable" because founder labor is treated as free.
- Sensitivity to price, conversion, churn, acquisition cost, salaries, and usage costs.

Show profitability separately for conservative, base, and upside scenarios. Identify whether the business can become sustainably profitable at a realistic scale, not merely whether revenue can exceed hosting costs.

### 10. Risks, constraints, and failure modes

Assess the most material risks, including:

- Weak or infrequent customer pain.
- Low willingness to pay or poor retention.
- Market too small, fragmented, or expensive to reach.
- Strong competitors, platform dependency, or easy replication.
- Long sales cycles, procurement friction, and cash-flow gaps.
- Technical feasibility, reliability, data protection, security, and AI-related risks where relevant.
- Regulatory, licensing, tax, employment, and cross-border obligations.
- Founder/team capability and execution capacity, using only information actually available.
- Dependence on a single channel, supplier, customer, or platform.
- Timing, seasonality, macroeconomic exposure, and likely changes in the market.

Rank risks by likelihood and impact. Explain what evidence would reduce or increase each risk.

### 11. Validation plan and decision gates

Design the cheapest sequence of tests that can disprove the idea before major investment.

Include:
- Customer interviews with a defined target profile and sample size rationale.
- Landing page, prototype, concierge MVP, paid pilot, pre-orders, or other suitable experiments.
- Pricing and willingness-to-pay tests.
- Acquisition-channel experiments.
- Concrete pass/fail thresholds, time limits, and maximum test budgets.
- Evidence required before moving from validation to MVP, launch, and scale.

Prefer behavioral evidence (payment, signed commitments, repeated use) over compliments, survey enthusiasm, or vanity metrics.

### 12. Go/no-go recommendation

Make an explicit recommendation:

- **GO:** evidence supports investment in the proposed plan.
- **CONDITIONAL GO:** proceed only with specified validation gates or changes.
- **PIVOT:** the problem may be real, but the proposed segment, product, market, pricing, or business model is weak.
- **NO-GO:** expected commercial return does not justify the required time, money, and risk.

State:
- The strongest reason to pursue it.
- The strongest reason to reject it.
- The assumptions most likely to invalidate the business case.
- The minimum evidence required to change the recommendation.
- Maximum sensible initial investment before validation.
- What should be built, tested, or deliberately not built next.
- Explicit kill criteria and a date or milestone for reassessment.

## Required financial presentation

Use a consistent currency and state whether figures exclude or include VAT/sales tax. For Romania, show RON and, where useful, EUR equivalents with the exchange-rate date and source.

Provide a scenario table with at least:

| Metric | Conservative | Base | Upside |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Time to launch | | | |
| MRR/revenue at 6 months | | | |
| MRR/revenue at 12 months | | | |

| Customers at 12 months | | | |
| Gross margin | | | |
| Monthly operating costs at 12 months | | | |
| Monthly marketing spend at 12 months | | | |

Adapt the horizons if the idea requires a different cycle, but explain why. Do not fill cells with invented precision. Use ranges where evidence is weak.

## Required final report structure

1. Mandatory probability sentence.
2. Verdict at a glance: GO / CONDITIONAL GO / PIVOT / NO-GO.
3. Assumptions and definition of the target MRR and timeframe.
4. Executive assessment: blunt conclusion in plain language.
5. Best market fit and geographic comparison.
6. Market size, customer demand, and evidence quality.
7. Customer, positioning, competition, and pricing.
8. MVP scope, development effort, cost, and timeline.
9. Conservative/base/upside revenue scenarios.
10. Operating cost and cash requirement scenarios.
11. Marketing strategy, budget, and advertising timeline.
12. Unit economics, profitability, and break-even.
13. Risks, constraints, and failure modes.
14. Validation experiments, budgets, thresholds, and decision gates.
15. Final go/no-go recommendation, kill criteria, and next actions.
16. Sources, data dates, assumptions, and unresolved evidence gaps.

## Tone and quality bar

- Be objective, skeptical, direct, and commercially pragmatic.
- Do not praise the user or the idea as a social courtesy.
- Do not confuse technical buildability with market demand or profitability.
- Do not assume that a large TAM means the business can capture meaningful revenue.
- Do not assume that a founder can acquire customers cheaply just because a product is digital.
- Do not hide unattractive conclusions behind vague language.
- Do not manufacture precision. Use ranges and confidence levels where appropriate.
- Make the reasoning auditable: show formulas, sources, assumptions, and why each conclusion follows.
- A clear NO-GO supported by evidence is a successful outcome of this analysis.
